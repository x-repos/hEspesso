In the advancing landscape of sustainable energy, the development of efficient and reversible hydrogen storage
materials operable under ambient conditions remains a critical challenge for material scientists and the broader
research  community.  Hydrogen,  owing  to  its  exceptionally  high  energy  density,  is  regarded  as  a  leading
candidate  for  facilitating  the  transition  from  conventional  fossil  fuels  to  cleaner,  renewable  energy  systems.
However, alongside its production, the safe and efficient storage of hydrogen presents a significant bottleneck
due to its low volumetric density and associated safety concerns.

Conventional storage techniques such as high-pressure compression and cryogenic liquefaction, though widely
used, demand complex infrastructure and carry substantial safety risks. These limitations have steered growing
interest toward solid-state hydrogen storage systems that rely on physisorption or chemisorption mechanisms,
preferably operating near ambient conditions. Consequently, the pursuit of materials with favourable thermo-
dynamics and kinetics for reversible hydrogen uptake and release has become imperative. Among the emerging
candidates, MXenes, a class of two-dimensional (2D) materials comprising of transition metal carbides, nitrides,
or carbonitrides have garnered significant attention due to their high surface area, tuneable surface chemistry,
and excellent conductivity.

Despite the growing body of literature on hydrogen storage using MXenes, a comprehensive evaluation that
bridges  the  gap  between  theoretical  predictions  and  experimental  realities  remains  limited.  This  review  ad-
dresses  that  gap  by  critically  examining  current  strategies  for  solid-state  hydrogen  storage,  with  a  particular
emphasis on MXene-based materials. It highlights the influence of synthesis techniques on structural properties,
discusses the mechanism of hydrogen interaction with MXene surface, and evaluates their practical implications
in real-world applications. While the potential of MXenes in hydrogen storage is considerable, it is not yet fully
realized. This article provides an in-depth assessment of the current advancements, challenges, and future di-
rections  for  MXene-based  materials  in  the  context  of  hydrogen  storage,  offering  valuable  insights  for  both
fundamental research and applied energy systems.

hydrogen is not yet a dominant energy source. First, hydrogen functions
solely  as  an  energy  carrier  rather  than  a  primary  energy  source.
Although it is the most abundant element in the universe, it does not
exist in its elemental form on Earth and must be produced from com-
pounds such as water or hydrocarbons. This means that energy must be
expended for its production, posing an economic challenge, especially
considering society’s long-standing reliance on inexpensive energy since
the onset of the industrial revolution.

Two-dimensional (2D) materials have recently gained considerable
attention as promising candidates for hydrogen storage applications due
to their unique physicochemical properties, including high surface-to-
volume  ratios,  excellent  thermal  conductivity,  and  superior  mechani-
cal  strength  [30–37].  Among  these,  MXenes  layered  structures
composed of transition metal carbides or nitrides have been extensively
investigated for a wide range of applications, such as electrochemical
CO2 reduction, electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, hydrogen
evolution  reactions  (HER),  supercapacitors,  batteries,  and  heteroge-
neous catalysis [21,38–41]. Given the well-established role of transition
metals  like  titanium  in  enhancing  the  hydrogen  storage  capacity  of
carbon-based  materials,  titanium-based  MXenes  offer  a  particularly
promising  platform  for  hydrogen  storage  [42].  The  incorporation  of
transition  metals  in  MXene  structures  enables  potential  hydrogen
adsorption  through  Kubas-type  interactions,  which  are  favourable  for
reversible  hydrogen  binding  [43–45].  Despite  their  theoretically  high
storage capacities, estimated to reach up to ~8.5 wt.% based on density
functional theory (DFT) calculations only a limited number of studies
have  explored
the  practical  hydrogen  storage  capabilities  of
MXene-based materials[46,47]. Notably, Ti2C MXene has exhibited an
exceptional hydrogen adsorption capacity of 8.6 wt.%, surpassing con-
ventional gravimetric capacities. According to a study of Hu et al., Ti2C
achieves a theoretical maximum capacity of 8.6 wt.%, contributed by
chemisorbed  hydrogen  (1.7  wt.%),  physisorbed  hydrogen  (3.4  wt.%),
and  hydrogen  bound  via  Kubas-type  interaction  (3.4  wt.%).  Impor-
tantly,  the  Kubas-type  interaction,  with  a  moderate  binding  energy
(~0.272 eV), enables reversible hydrogen storage under near-ambient
conditions [48].

This paper presents a comprehensive review of recent advancements
in  hydrogen  storage  technologies  utilizing  MXene-based  composites.
The primary objective of this work is to highlight the key properties of
MXene-derived  materials  and  their  application  potential  in  hydrogen
storage systems. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first detailed
effort  to  systematically  analyse  MXene-based  materials  for  hydrogen
storage,  encompassing  their  synthesis,  functional  performance,  and
critical  safety  considerations  during  hydrogen  handling  and  storage.
Various synthesis strategies for MXene composites are discussed, along
with an evaluation of their efficiency in facilitating hydrogen uptake and
release. Additionally, the review outlines future prospects and potential
solutions that MXene-based materials offer for the development of next-
generation hydrogen storage technologies.

1.1. MXenes

Since the discovery of MXenes in 2011 by Gogotsi and his co-workers
at Drexel University, this emerging class of two-dimensional transition
metal-based material  has garnered significant attention  for its unique
physicochemical properties and wide application spectrum. MXenes are
generally  represented  by  the  formula  Mn+1XnTx  (where  n  = 1–3),  in
which M is an early transition metal, X is carbon and/or nitrogen, and Tx

Ti3C2Tx  is  the  most  widely  studied  MXene,  representing  the  carbide
family, an increasingly diverse range of compositions, including metal
nitrides (e.g., Ti2N, Nb2N) and carbonitrides (e.g., Ti3CN, Ti2CN), have
been synthesized or predicted. These variants exhibit distinct structural,
electronic, and surface characteristics, expanding the functional utility
of  MXenes in  energy-related applications, including hydrogen storage
and production [51–53]. The distance between the layers of MXenes is
called the interlayer distance, and it is mostly measured in nanometres
(nm),  depending  on  the  value  of  n.  The  pristine  MXenes’  layered
structures  and  two-dimensional  morphology  facilitate  the  straightfor-
ward  creation  of  composites  with  other  materials  to  improve  and
develop new properties [40]. The variation in the X-component (carbon,
nitrogen, or a combination thereof) within MXene structures critically
governs  their  interlayer  spacing,  surface  functionalization,  and  elec-
tronic charge distribution. In particular, nitride-based MXenes exhibit
distinct  electronic  characteristics,  including  a  higher  localization  of
states near the Fermi level, attributable to the greater electronegativity
of  nitrogen  compared  to  carbon.  This  intrinsic  electronic  polarity  en-
hances their interaction with hydrogen species, thereby promoting more
favourable adsorption energies and facilitating efficient proton-coupled
electron transfer processes. Additionally, the robust chemical and ther-
mal  stability  exhibited  by  nitride  MXenes  under  extreme  conditions
renders  them  highly  suitable  for  applications  in  catalysis  and

electrochemical systems where both reactivity and structural durability
are required.

Carbonitride  MXenes,  incorporating  both  carbon  and  nitrogen  in
their X-layer, exhibit a synergistic combination of the desirable prop-
erties found in carbide and nitride MXenes. They retain the mechanical
integrity and conductivity of carbides while gaining enhanced surface
reactivity  and  electronic  flexibility  from  nitrogen  incorporation.  This
dual character enables intermediate hydrogen binding energies, which
are optimal for reversible hydrogen storage. The mixed C/N chemistry
also  introduces  controllable  defect  sites  and  electronic  heterogeneity,
enhancing  hydrogen  spill  over  and  surface  interactions.  Additionally,
the intrinsic 2D layered morphology and hydrophilic nature of MXenes
support  rapid ion  transport and  expose  a  high density of  active  sites.
Their interlayer spacing, tuneable via precursor selection or intercala-
tion plays a pivotal role in determining hydrogen uptake capacity. Both
theoretical  and  experimental  studies  affirm  that  adjusting  the  M/X
stoichiometry and surface terminations significantly influences adsorp-
tion energetics, positioning nitride and carbonitride MXenes as highly
promising alternatives to traditional carbide MXenes in hydrogen stor-
age technologies [54–56].

2.1. Physical method for hydrogen storage

Physical methods of hydrogen storage primarily include compressed
gas  and  cryogenic  liquid  storage  systems  [89].  In  Type  IV  carbon
fiber-reinforced composite cylinders, hydrogen is stored in its gaseous
form at high pressures ranging from 35 to 70 MPa. Given that the energy
density  of  hydrogen  gas  at  ambient  temperature and  pressure  is  only
about  10  MJ/m3,  specialized  tanks  made  of  advanced  alloys  or  com-
posite  materials  are required to  ensure safe  and  efficient storage. For
example, at a pressure of 700 bar, a single storage tank with a volumetric
energy density of approximately 2.9 MJ/L and a gravimetric capacity of
4.2 wt.% can be employed, with an estimated cost of around $15 per
kWh [90]. One of the major advantages of high-pressure gas storage is

2.2. Chemical method for hydrogen storage

In this hydrogen storage technology, hydrogen is stored through the
formation  of  covalent  bonds  with  liquid  or  solid-state  materials.  This
method  typically  involves  catalytic  processes  that  decompose  source
materials to chemically store hydrogen, often in the form of compounds
such  as  ammonia.  Ammonia-based  compounds  have  demonstrated
hydrogen  storage  capacities  of  up  to  17.4  wt.%  [43].  Materials
commonly  used  for  this  type  of  chemical  hydrogen  storage  include
ammonia, liquid organic heterocycles, hydrocarbons (such as benzene
and methane), and ammonia borane (AB) [112]. At room temperature,
low silica type X zeolites with alkali metal cations have demonstrated
hydrogen storage capabilities of 1.6 wt.%. The most popular substances
for  chemical hydrogen  storage are  metal  hydrides [113]. A hydrogen
storage  capacity  of  up  to  7.6  wt.%  was  established  using  magnesium
hydride  (MgH2),  significantly  above  the  DOE  requirement.  Complex
hydrides,  such  as  lithium  and  sodium  borohydride,  have  also  been
extensively  studied  and  have  been  shown  to  have  storage  capacities
ranging from 15 to 19 wt.% [114]. Despite their high storage potential,
these chemical systems often suffer from slow kinetics and require high
temperatures  for  both  hydrogenation  and  dehydrogenation,  posing
significant challenges for reversible and practical hydrogen storage ap-
plications [115].

3. Interaction involved in hydrogen storage

Hydrogen storage in solid-state materials generally occurs through
two  primary  mechanisms:  weak  physisorption  of  molecular  hydrogen
and  strong  chemisorption  involving  dissociated  hydrogen  atoms.  In
physisorption, hydrogen molecules are bound to the material surface via
van der Waals forces with binding energies typically below 0.1 eV. In
contrast, chemisorption involves the formation of strong covalent bonds
between  dissociated  hydrogen  atoms  and  metal  atoms,  with  binding
energies usually less than 0.8 eV. An intermediate interaction, known as
the Kubas interaction, offers a balance between these two extremes. In
this  case,  molecular  hydrogen  interacts  with  transition  metal  centres
through  a  reversible  coordination  mechanism,  with  binding  energies
typically in the range of 0.2–0.8 eV per H2 molecule. This energy range is
considered ideal for reversible hydrogen storage under ambient pressure
and  moderate  temperatures  (300–400  K),  making  Kubas-type  in-
teractions  particularly  attractive  for  practical  storage  applications
[116]. Kubas-type binding interactions are considered highly promising
for  hydrogen  storage,  as  their  binding  energies  lie  between  those  of
physisorption  and  chemisorption,  providing  an  optimal  balance  for
reversible  adsorption.  In  this  mechanism,  electron  donation  from  the
H–H σ-bonding orbital to an empty d-orbital of a transition metal occurs,
resulting in the elongation of the H–H bond without complete dissoci-
ation. This unique interaction facilitates molecular hydrogen adsorption
with moderate bond strength, making it suitable for ambient-condition
hydrogen  storage  applications  [109].  Concurrently,  π-back-donation
from  the  filled  d-orbitals  of  the  transition  metal  to  the  empty  σ*

Numerous efforts have been made to enhance the binding energy be-

5. Revelation for future

As  the  global  energy  landscape  transitions  toward  sustainable  al-
ternatives, hydrogen stands out as a compelling synthetic fuel due to its
high specific energy and compatibility with renewable energy produc-
tion cycles. With the potential to be generated from renewable sources in
a  closed-loop  system,  hydrogen  offers  a  path  toward  a  zero-emission
energy economy. Much like fossil fuels once revolutionized the indus-
trial era, hydrogen is expected to drive the next wave of technological
and economic advancement.

Future  hydrogen  storage  technologies  must  be  tailored  for  both
stationary and mobile applications, each presenting unique design and
performance challenges. Despite several  established hydrogen storage
strategies  such  as  compressed  gas,  liquefied  hydrogen,  and  metal  hy-
drides,  significant  innovation  is  still  required  to  address  limitations
related  to  energy  density,  safety,  cost,  and  kinetics.  Material  science,
therefore, plays a pivotal role in identifying and optimizing novel stor-
age media with enhanced sorption performance and thermal stability.

MXene-based materials, as highlighted in this review, have emerged
as  highly  versatile  candidates  for  solid-state  hydrogen  storage  due  to
their  unique  structure,  surface  tunability,  and  catalytic  capabilities.
Their ability to facilitate both physical and chemical adsorption mech-
anisms;  including  physisorption,  chemisorption,  and  Kubas-type  in-
teractions under near-ambient conditions presents a distinct advantage
over  traditional  materials.  Furthermore,  MXene  composites  have

6. Conclusion

In  this  review  article,  the  authors  have  summarized  the  key  chal-
lenges  associated  with  hydrogen  storage  technologies  and  discussed
various  strategies  currently  employed  to  store  hydrogen  through
different  mechanisms.  Among  the  emerging  materials,  MXene-based
compounds have demonstrated significant potential as alternative can-
didates  for enhancing hydrogen  storage capacity. These  materials are
capable of storing hydrogen up to 8.8 wt.% gravimetrically and 6.4 wt.%
reversibly.  Such  high  storage  capacities  highlight  the  suitability  of
MXene-based materials for reversible hydrogen storage applications.

To further improve the performance and applicability of MXenes in
hydrogen storage, future research can focus on the following directions:

(a)  Optimizing  the  interlayer  distance  within  MXene  layers  can
significantly improve hydrogen adsorption capacity by providing
more accessible sites for storage.

(b)  Surface  functional  groups  play  a  critical  role  in  hydrogen
adsorption by facilitating interactions between hydrogen mole-
cules and the MXene surface. Therefore, refining synthesis tech-
niques  and  conducting  in-depth  studies  on  the  influence  and
behavior of functional groups are essential.

(c)  The  interaction  of  atomic  or  molecular  hydrogen  with  MXene
surfaces  via  mechanisms  such  as  physisorption,  chemisorption,
and Kubas-type binding is vital for effective storage. A thorough
understanding  of  MXene’s  surface  topography  and  electronic
properties is necessary to optimize these interactions.

(d)  Incorporating metal atoms into the MXene structure may enhance
hydrogen  adsorption  through  electronic  and  structural  modifi-
cations, thereby improving storage capacity.

(e)  Synthesizing  MXene-based  composites  with  enhanced  porosity
and surface area can lead to better hydrogen uptake, making this
a promising area for further exploration.

(f)  More  research  should  be  directed  toward  developing  materials
that utilize Kubas-type interactions, which offer an intermediate
binding  energy  suitable  for  reversible  hydrogen  storage  at
ambient conditions.

To  develop  materials  with  high  physisorption  capacity,  various
